Abstract. Infrared thermography is gaining attention as an affordable, portable,
privacy-sensitive, and radiation-free breast imaging modality. However, thermal
images alone provide only two-dimensional (2D) information necessitates mul-
tiple images to visualize the complete 180° breast region, posing challenges in
visually correlating these images. To address this issue, we propose 3D-BreastNet,
a deep-learning architecture that reconstructs 3D thermal images from 2D ther-
mal images. The proposed method employs a self-supervised learning strategy
to learn the 3D silhouette and overlay observed temperatures onto the predicted
3D structure. To evaluate the performance of 3D-BreastNet, we compared the 2D
projections of the predicted 3D breast silhouette at standard view angles with their
corresponding input 2D silhouettes. The accuracy, dice index, jaccard index, and
hausdorff distance were found to be 0.97, 0.97, 0.94, and 3.86, respectively, on an
independent test set comprising 1000 thermal images from 200 participants. The
results obtained with 3D-BreastNet demonstrate promising outcomes, highlight-
ing the feasibility of reconstructing 3D temperature information from 2D thermal
images alone. This research opens new possibilities for enhanced breast imaging
and diagnosis using deep learning techniques.

1 Introduction
Infrared thermography has gained recognition as a valuable tool for medical imaging,
offering beneﬁts such as non-invasiveness, affordability, and radiation-free imaging. It
has been used for various applications, including breast cancer diagnosis, treatment mon-
itoring, and pain management [ 1]. In recent times, infrared thermography has emerged
as a potential screening tool for COVID-19 in public places like airports and hospitals
[2]. Advancements in uncooled microbolometer Focal Plane Arrays (FPAs) have played
a pivotal role in re-establishing the use of infrared thermography in medical applications
since the 1990s. Modern thermal imaging cameras can detect temperature variations as
low as 20 mK with high spatial resolution and are available at affordable costs [ 3].

Breast cancer is a major cause of cancer-related deaths among women globally [ 4].
Early detection is crucial for improving survival rates, but low- and middle-income coun-
tries face challenges in implementing conventional imaging modalities like mammogra-
phy, MRI, and ultrasound due to cost and infrastructure constraints [5–8]. The increased
metabolic activity in cancerous tissues leads to elevated heat generation, which can be
detected through thermal imaging [9–13]. By leveraging the advantages of affordability
and portability, thermal imaging has the potential to serve as an alternative modality for
breast cancer detection, particularly in resource-constrained settings [ 14]. Breast ther-
mography has been vastly explored in the literature for breast cancer diagnosis [ 15, 16],
treatment monitoring [12, 17], prognosis [18], breast augmentation [19], and 3D bioheat
modeling of cancerous regions.
While thermal imaging provides valuable two-dimensional (2D) temperature distri-
bution information, it lacks the spatial characteristics of three-dimensional (3D) thermal
patterns. To overcome this limitation, multiple 2D thermal views are captured to cover
the entire breast region. However, visually correlating these images poses challenges,
requiring mental mapping of large amounts of pixel information. The availability of 3D
spatial information is crucial for accurate tumor depth estimation, 3D bio-heat modeling,
tumor orientation analysis, structural property assessment, and breast augmentation.
Existing research on the automated generation of 3D thermal images from 2D ther-
mal images is limited. Some approaches utilize additional hardware [ 20–24], such as
3D scanners, which increase complexity and imaging concurrence requirements. Other
methods rely on image processing techniques [ 25] that approximate 3D shapes from
body contours alone, potentially resulting in imperfect 3D reconstructions due to the
non-rigid and amorphous nature of the breast. In recent years, deep learning [26–28] has
shown promising results in 3D object and scene reconstruction from 2D images with-
out explicit 3D ground truth. These approaches leverage information about captured
view angles and spatial distances between pixels to infer the missing dimension. Build-
ing upon these advances, we propose a deep learning framework called 3D-BreastNet
to reconstruct 3D thermal images of the breast surface from 2D thermal images cap-
tured at different view angles, without relying on 3D ground truth or precise view angle
information.
Standard breast thermography protocols involve capturing multiple 2D thermal
images at various view angles to cover the 180° breast region. However, challenges
arise in capturing images at the precise standard view angles due to practical clinical
settings, patient movement, and communication issues. With the promising results of
deep learning-based 3D reconstruction in other imaging domains, it becomes feasible
to generate 3D temperature information of the breast surface using deep learning solely
from 2D thermal images. This paper presents the 3D-BreastNet framework as a solution
to reconstruct 3D thermal images of the breast surface from 2D thermal images captured
at different view angles, overcoming challenges associated with standard view angle
deviations and the lack of 3D ground truth.

2 Methods
2.1 Dataset Description
We collected an anonymized dataset comprising thermal images from 2027 participants
in collaboration with multiple hospitals and diagnostic centers in India. Ethical approvals
were obtained from the respective institutional review boards, and participants provided
signed informed consent forms. For each participant, we captured ﬁve thermal images
at standard view angles: right lateral (RL) at − 90°, right oblique (RO) at − 45°, frontal
(F) at 0°, left oblique (LO) at 45°, and left lateral (LL) at 90° (illustrated in Fig. 1(a)).
The thermal imaging protocol involved several steps. Firstly, participants were cooled
in an air-conditioned room maintained at a temperature range of 22 °C to 24 °C to
eliminate extraneous heat patterns prior to imaging. We utilized a ﬁxed rotating chair
with position markers to facilitate easy and comfortable rotation of the participants.
The camera height and distance were adjusted individually for each participant, and
verbal instructions were given to ensure participants turned to the standard view angles
for image capture. To minimize translational and zoom variances, both the camera and
chair remained ﬁxed throughout the imaging process for each participant.
Although we followed the imaging protocol diligently, the collected thermal images
deviated slightly from the standard view angles due to the inherent challenges of captur-
ing images precisely at those angles. We employed the FLIR E75 (FLIR, Wilsonville,
US) thermal camera, which offers a thermal sensitivity of 50 mK, a spatial resolution of
320 × 240, and a ﬁeld of view of 24° × 18°.
2.2 Proposed Methodology
Traditional supervised algorithms for 3D breast surface reconstruction typically require
3D breast thermal surfaces as ground truth, which are challenging to obtain due to the
absence of publicly available datasets. To address the limitations of requiring labeled
datasets with 3D ground truth, we propose a self-supervised deep learning framework
named 3D-BreastNet, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
The 3D-BreastNet framework consists of ﬁve main steps: i) foreground segmenta-
tion from input thermal images to segment 2D breast silhouettes, ii) 3D breast silhou-
ette generation from the segmented 2D breast silhouettes, iii) a 2D view generator to
obtain random 2D silhouette projections from the generated 3D breast silhouette, iv)
loss function to minimize the distance between the generated 2D projections and the
actual silhouettes of the input 2D thermal images, and v) overlay function to map the
input temperature values onto the generated 3D silhouette (3D volume).
2.2.1 Foreground Segmentation
To extract the breast region from the input thermal images, a binary segmentation process
is employed using Otsu’s method. This converts the raw temperature values into binary
images, where ‘0’ represents the background pixels and ‘1’ represents the foreground
pixels. This segmentation process is applied to each of the ﬁve thermal viewsT

2.2.2 3D Silhouette Generator
The goal of the 3D silhouette generator is to learn a reconstruction functionG : R2 → R3,
that generates the 3D silhouette of the participant based on the ﬁve binary 2D silhouettes.
We employ an encoder-decoder CNN architecture, as proposed in [ 26], to accomplish
this task. The architecture consists of ﬁve up-sampling and ﬁve down-sampling stages,
followed by a sigmoid activation layer to generate the 3D breast silhouette. The ﬁve
2D silhouettes obtained from the foreground segmentation are stacked along the z-axis
(channels) to form the input to the encoder.

2.2.5 Overlay
After achieving loss convergence and obtaining the 3D breast silhouette, overlaying
the temperature values is performed for 3D temperature reconstruction. To ensure the
preservation of actual temperature values, the overlay of input temperature values onto
the generated 3D silhouette is performed as an additional step instead of directly learning
the 3D temperature surfaces with 3D-BreastNet. For each input 2D thermal image, the
estimated view angle is obtained by identifying the view angle that corresponds to the
minimum Dice loss between its 2D silhouette image and different projections of the
generated 3D breast silhouette. This estimated view angle compensates for deviations
from the standard view angles in the captured images. The generated 3D silhouette is
then rotated to the estimated view angle, and the corresponding 2D temperatures from
the input images are added to the 3D silhouettes, resulting in the generation of 3D breast
surface temperatures. In cases where multiple temperatures are added to the same point,
the average temperature is considered. Figure 1 illustrates the interactions between the
different modules involved in generating the 3D temperature overlay image.
3 Experimentation and Results
The dataset consisted of thermal data collected from 2027 participants, which was ran-
domly divided into training, validation, and independent test sets with 1627, 200, and
200 participants, respectively. The random seed used for the split was kept consistent
38 A. P . Saha et al.
throughout the experimentation process. Each participant’s thermal data comprised ﬁve
captured thermal images corresponding to the standard views. These thermal images
were represented as arrays of raw temperature values with dimensions of 320 × 240.
To normalize the temperature values within a range of [0, 1], each participant’s
thermal images were ﬁrst normalized using their corresponding minimum and maximum
temperatures. Subsequently, foreground segmentation was applied to the normalized
images to obtain the 2D silhouette images. These images from different views were
concatenated to form the input for the 3D silhouette generator architecture. All images
were resized to 64 × 64 to match the input dimensions of the 3D silhouette generator.
The implementation of the 3D silhouette generator (G) employed an encoder-
decoder architecture. The encoder consisted of ﬁve blocks of convolutional and batch-
normalization layers, while the decoder included ﬁve blocks of 3D transposed convo-
lutional and 3D batch-normalization layers. The encoder performed down-sampling to
map the stacked thermal images to a ﬂattened latent code, which was a 1000-dimensional
vector. The decoder, operating in tandem with the encoder, mapped the latent code to
the 3D reconstruction model. The ﬁnal output of the decoder was a 3D volume with
dimensions 64 × 64 × 64.
The Dice loss function described in Eq. ( 7) was used to penalize the 3D silhouette
generator (G) when the random 2D projections of the predicted 3D silhouette, obtained
from a uniform distribution, deviated signiﬁcantly from the input 2D thermal images.
For experimentation, we set the number of random renderings per view (n) to 2 and the
batch size (B) to 16 subjects. The Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.025 and
decay rates of 0.5 and 0.9 was used for loss optimization. The network was trained for
400 epochs, allowing the network to learn from a diverse range of random projections
of the generated 3D silhouette.

400 epochs, demonstrating the convergence of the Dice loss and afﬁrming the learning
capability of the proposed 3D-BreastNet.

4 Discussion
Breast thermography offers several advantages, including low cost, non-invasiveness,
and portability. Recent research [ 1–3, 9–19] has demonstrated its potential as an alter-
native imaging modality, particularly when standard imaging techniques like mammog-
raphy are unavailable. Three-dimensional thermal imaging plays a crucial role in breast
diagnosis [ 3, 16], 3D bio-heat simulation [ 15], understanding tumor properties [ 32,
33], and breast augmentation [ 19], enabling better visualization. By reconstructing 3D
temperature information from 2D thermal images alone, additional hardware for cap-
turing depth information is not required. However, generating 3D breast thermal images
presents various challenges. Unlike visual images, thermal images lack strong scale-
invariant features, and obtaining precise 3D ground truth breast surfaces for supervised
learning is difﬁcult. Moreover, imperfect image capture and lack of information on the
exact view angle further complicate the problem.
Our proposed solution, 3D-BreastNet, leverages the strengths of deep learning to
address these challenges. It generates the 3D breast thermal surface from 2D thermal
images without requiring explicit view angles or actual 3D ground truth. To assess the
accuracy of 3D-BreastNet, we compared random 2D projections of the 3D silhouette at
standard angles with the input images. While, in a real scenario, comparing predicted
2D projections at each angle with actual 2D projections would be ideal, we considered
comparisons at the ﬁve standard view angles at which the images were captured. This

approach provides a holistic view of performance, as these angles are widely spaced,
making it difﬁcult for the 3D generator to overﬁt to these speciﬁc view angles without
disregarding rotational geometry constraints.

Our main hypothesis in reconstructing 3D breast thermal silhouettes from 2D thermal
images is that the Dice loss between the 2D projections of the 3D breast silhouette and the
input 2D silhouette increases when the projection angle deviates signiﬁcantly from the
captured view angle. With this assumption, we trained G for longer epochs to minimize
the Dice loss between the input image and its neighbouring random views, aiming for the
loss to be optimal when the expected predicted random slices of the volume closely match
the actual input image for that view angle. To validate the hypothesis, we calculated the
expected (mean) Dice loss for each standard view by comparing the 2D silhouettes of
thermal images in the independent test set with different projections of the generated
3D silhouette volumes at view angles ranging from − 90° to +90° at every 1° interval.
The graphs in Fig. 3 illustrate that the average Dice loss was at its minimum when the
rendered 2D projections were close to the standard view angles (0°, ±45°, and ±90°).
By examining Fig. 3, we determined that the average Dice loss was lowest at 4°, − 80°,
+81°, − 44°, and +41° on the test dataset for frontal (0°), right lateral (− 90°), left lateral
(+90°), right oblique ( − 45°), and left oblique (+45°) views, respectively. These angles
slightly deviated from the standard view angles, and we believe they represent the mean
view angles at which the input images were captured, accounting for the challenges
faced during image capture.

ability of 3D-BreastNet to generate 3D structural volumes that closely resemble true 3D
volumes. Additionally, as shown in Fig.4(c), the rendered 2D projections of the 3D breast

Overall, the proposed methodology of 3D-BreastNet yields reasonably good results,
but it has a few limitations. One signiﬁcant limitation is the lack of restrictions on the
breast shapes of the generated volume. As a result, the predicted structures may appear
distorted in some cases, as shown in Fig. 4(b). The breast surface can be assumed to
closely resemble a semi-ellipsoidal structure. In our future work, we plan to incorporate
this shape prior into the generator network to reduce deformations in the predicted
volumes. We also intend to explore the adoption of concatenating encoder outputs with
decoder inputs, as proposed in U-Net [ 34], to improve the generation of 3D breast
silhouettes.
Three-dimensional thermal imaging is equally important for imaging other body
parts, such as the hand, face, and knee [ 20, 35]. The challenges of obtaining 3D labeled
ground truth, requiring additional hardware, and lacking precise view angle information
are common in the 3D reconstruction of these body parts as well. Since our proposed
3D-BreastNet can alleviate these issues, we believe the algorithm can be extended to

5 Conclusion
In conclusion, thermography for breast imaging is gaining renewed attention due to its
numerous advantages, including affordability, non-invasiveness, and portability. How-
ever, it’s important to note that thermal images only provide a two-dimensional view
of the breast surface temperature. To overcome this limitation, our 3D-BreastNet aims
to generate three-dimensional (3D) surface temperatures from two-dimensional (2D)
thermal images. Notably, this is the ﬁrst approach that achieves 3D breast surface image
generation from 2D breast thermograms without requiring additional imaging hardware
or precise view angle information. The proposed self-supervised loss function is innova-
tive and holds potential for generating 3D surfaces with other imaging modalities. Future
work should focus on incorporating shape priors into the deep learning model, which
will further enhance its capabilities. Overall, our research presents a promising direc-
tion for improving breast imaging through the generation of 3D surface temperatures,
opening doors for more accurate and comprehensive analysis of breast health.
